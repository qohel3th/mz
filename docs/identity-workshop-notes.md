## 1. Workshop philosophy and terminology

### Core thesis (Introduction, p.1)

> "A great life begins when a person takes a stand on who he wants to become, what he wants to serve, and what his effort must look like."
> "This requires two changes:
> First, radical identity change.
> Then, radical behavior change."
> "First things first: you must make a commitment.
> You must decide, with immense seriousness, on the kind of person you want to become.
> This commitment will become your personal religion — the standard that judges your behavior."
> "Once the commitment is made, you must immediately cement it through action. You must teach your nervous system: this is who we are now. This is what we do now. This is where my strength lies. This is where my greatness lies."
> "But the nervous system is a slow learner. It learns by simple and unfanciful repetition."
> "You wouldn't go to the gym once and say, "Wonderful, I now have a great body.""
> "Only through long-term, repeated action will you come to any kind of success."
> "If you can stay in service of your ideal self — slowly, stubbornly, day after day — you will generate a powerful momentum that begins to carry and ennoble you. Your devotion builds a wave, and eventually that wave starts to carry you."
> "You're no longer searching for meaning and purpose, you're actively creating them."

### "From vague aspiration to clear direction" (p.1)

> "The goal behind these assignments is to make your aspiration concrete. Before you can practice seriously, you have to clarify what you're practicing toward."

The intro then previews each assignment's logic (all verbatim):

- **Ideal self:** "When you identify your ideal self, you're implicitly naming the values and ideals you hold most dearly — the image of human excellence you most want to grow toward."
- **What pulls you:** "When you identify what pulls you, you're locating the sources of your vitality and strength. The places where life gives you unusual energy and power. You're paying attention to the people, activities, problems, crafts, subjects, causes and ways of life that call to you."
- **Life rules:** "When you write your life rules, you're documenting the conditions under which you flourish or decline. You're turning your life experiences into hard-won lessons, and hard-won lessons into laws you can live by."
- **Work life audit:** "When you audit your work life, you separate the work itself from the conditions surrounding it. You identify which tasks energize you, which tasks drain you, whether the work feels meaningful, and which conditions make it better or worse. This helps you make practical decisions: what to do more of, what to do less of, what skills to build, what conversations to have, what can be improved and whether it is time to move on to something else."
- **Road / work thesis:** "When you identify which road you're on and write your one-line work thesis, you're clarifying your path toward fulfilling work. You're compressing your interests and values into a clear direction. Some people begin with passion and must learn to make it practical. Others begin with practical work and must slowly bend it toward passion."
- "These are only starting points, but they're powerful ones."
- "Taken together, these assignments begin the work of self-actualization."

### Stage 1 — "1st assignment: Identify two to three ideal selves"

**Exact name in PDF:** "1st assignment" / "Identify two to three ideal selves"

**Purpose / logic:** "Great behavior change comes from great identity change. We rise or fall to match the picture we have of ourselves." Comes first because identity change precedes behavior change. Why 2–3: "You are not one person, however. You are not a single personality. You're a constellation." / "if you confine your identity to only one self, you risk suffocating or restricting yourself." / "That's why it's helpful to identify two to three ideal selves. You're giving your soul room to roam."

Epigraph (verbatim): "I can focus for 12 hours per day because I'm living my myth. Not because I have the best productivity routine... You can't force focus. Focus is triggered by interest. Interest is ignited by a story. Your personal myth is that story. And you're the only one who can write it." — David Bajramovic

The "sides" listed (verbatim, useful as inspiration chips):
- "an extroverted and socially savvy side"
- "a contemplative or philosophical side"
- "a spiritual or religious side"
- "an ambitious, builder-oriented side"
- "a nurturing, relational side"
- "an artistic or creative side"

**Prompt questions (verbatim):**
- "Name two or three ideal selves that deeply inspire you."
- "For each ideal self, write two to three sentences describing who this person is."
- "Now write your own."
- "My first ideal self is:"
- "My second ideal self is:"
- "My third ideal self is:"

**Constraints (verbatim):**
- Count: "two to three ideal selves" (min 2, max 3).
- "These are not fantasies about status, pleasure or wealth. They are visions of the kind of person you want to be."
- "Your ideal selves might be very different from one another (the scholar, the comedian, the beloved family man), or they might be variations within one creative domain (the painter, the writer, the sculptor)."
- Description length: "two to three sentences".
- Tense/voice: "Use the present tense." / "Don't write, "I want to become someone who…"" / "Write, "I am…""
- Example names: "The cultural critic", "The great family man", "The scholar", "The mystic", "The motivational public speaker", "The novelist", "The craftsman", "The community leader".
- Example descriptions (verbatim):
  - **The Community Leader** — "I bring people together, create spaces of belonging and help organize projects that improve the lives of others. I'm known for my reliability, helpfulness and the ability to hold a group together around a shared purpose."
  - **The Cultural Critic** — "Deeply observant and sardonic, I have my finger on the pulse of culture. I produce sharp, insightful commentaries on literature, media and society. I'm known for seeing patterns others miss and expressing them in ways that are both intellectually serious and engaging to a broad audience."
  - **The Scholar** — "I live a life organized around study and scholarly discussion. I read seriously, think carefully and am committed to mastering important ideas. I'm both intellectually serious and socially perceptive, able to translate complex ideas into vivid, engaging analysis."

### Stage 3 — "3rd assignment: Identify what pulls you"

**Exact name in PDF:** "3rd assignment" / "Identify what pulls you". Sub-sections: "1. Receptive Attractions" and "2. Participatory Attractions".

**Purpose / logic (verbatim):** "A meaningful life begins with noticing what already pulls you. Most people look for purpose as if it will arrive as a clear command from heaven. But often your purpose has already been speaking to you for years — through your fascinations, your recurring interests, your private zones of aliveness, the people you admire, the projects you lose yourself in, the topics you love researching." / "Your task is to become a careful student of your own attractions." / "Ask yourself: "Where has life already been calling me?"" Follows the ideal-self work because it grounds the aspiration in evidence from lived experience.

Distinction (verbatim): "Receptive attractions are the things that move you, inspire you, or awaken your admiration when you encounter them. These are very important." / "Just as important, however, are your participatory attractions. How do you love to participate in reality?" / "The subject is the same; the mode of participation is different." / "So pay attention to both: moments that moved you and moments where you loved participating in reality."

Participation verbs offered (verbatim): "Maybe you love explaining things. Maybe you love protecting people. Maybe you love beautifying spaces. Maybe you love solving technical problems. Maybe you love one-on-one conversations. Maybe you love making things. Maybe you love performing. Maybe you love children. Maybe you love argumentation. Maybe you love movement."

"Real-World Examples" (summaries, PDF numbering): 1. "The podcaster who imitated Mike Wallace" 2. "The actor inspired by Captain Hook" (Simon Callow) 3. "The accountant who loved cooking".

**Prompt questions (verbatim):**
- "Ask yourself: "Where has life already been calling me?""
- Receptive: "List three to five moments when something you encountered deeply moved, inspired or fascinated you."
- "My receptive attractions are:"
- Participatory: "List three to five moments when you felt alive because of what you were doing. Moments where you loved participating in reality."
- "My participatory attractions are:" (followed by numbered slots "1. 2. 3.")

**Constraints:**
- Receptive: 3–5 items. Examples (verbatim): "An essay or video that spoke to you"; "A hero or role model who inspired you"; "A place, atmosphere or way of life that made you think, "I want to live closer to that""; "A work of art, song, film scene or performance that stayed with you"; "A movie, book or TV character who moved you".

Examples (verbatim): "A school or work project that absorbed you deeply"; "A conversation that lit you up"; "A topic you repeatedly research, Google or try to understand more deeply"; "A time you loved organizing, hosting, leading or bringing people together"; "A time you loved making, fixing, designing, performing or competing".

### Stage 5 — "5th assignment: Write your life rules"

**Exact name in PDF:** "5th assignment" / "Write your life rules". Sub-sections: "1. Things I Must Avoid" and "2. Things I Must Have".

**Purpose / logic (verbatim):** "Socrates said the unexamined life is not worth living. Philosopher William Vallicella adds that the undocumented life is not worth examining. Closing (verbatim): "Don't rush this. Think from experience. Think from regret. Think from the days when you felt disgusting. ### Stage 7 — "6th assignment: Answer the poll + write your one-line work thesis"

**Exact name in PDF:** "6th assignment" / "Answer the poll + write your one-line work thesis". Prerequisite: "Before doing this assignment, make sure you've read through the lecture: "How to find your calling"" (lecture text is included in the PDF, pp.8–13).

**Purpose / logic (verbatim):** "Again and again, I meet two kinds of people: creatives who are deeply passionate about their studies or art, but who are broke or burned out because they can't make a living from it; and successful professionals, trapped in alienating work, who would give anything to develop a passion." / "Most career advice fails because it gives both of them the same prescription — when their problems are opposite." / ""Follow your passion" is excellent advice for one and paralyzing for the other." Moves from inner identity to outer work direction; "compressing your interests and values into a clear direction."

Lecture structure (headings verbatim): "Road one — Follow your passion, and build worldly value around it"; "Examples of road one"; "Cues that this is your road"; "The dangers of road one"; "Road two — Marry a problem you care about to a skill you enjoy, and watch passion grow"; "1— Name issues you care about"; "2— Name the work you enjoy doing"; "3— Form career hypotheses"; "Examples of road two"; "Cues that this is your road"; "The danger of road two"; "Both roads lead to fulfillment"; "How do I know if I'm on the right path?".

Road one cues (verbatim):
1. "You don't just enjoy the activity; you're drawn to the whole world around it — its heroes, its community, its history, its standards. You picture yourself as a member of that world."
2. "The pull matters more than any success or external reward."
3. "When you imagine "free time" away from it, the concept feels slightly nonsensical — work and play have collapsed into each other."

Road one dangers: "mistaken passion — believing you're on road one when you're not" (test: "do you really lose yourself in the world of the thing, or only in the contrast it makes with the rest of your life?"); and "passion without proper execution" ("You have to learn two crafts — how to do the thing well, and how to reliably get it in front of people in a valuable way.").

Road two cues (verbatim):
1. "When you ask yourself "what's my passion?" you come up either blank or with a list — several interests, none of them a clear summons."
2. "Your interests have shifted noticeably over the last five or ten years."
3. "You suspect that the thing you currently call a "passion" might just be "the most enjoyable corner of my current life" rather than a genuine vocation."
4. "You could imagine loving many different forms of work, provided the work was meaningful, challenging and allowed you to become excellent."

Road two danger: "meaningful work that is boring." / "You're looking for a field that matters to you and whose actual work engages you."

Road two sub-steps (Probably Good; verbatim prompts — these are optional pre-work in the lecture, not part of the assignment form):
- "1— Name issues you care about": "What issues, causes, or human problems have stayed with you over time? What do you find yourself reading about, talking about, or wishing someone would address? Which groups of people do you feel especially drawn to help?" Consider: "Injustices that reliably anger or disturb you."; "People or communities whose lives you would like to improve."; "Parts of society you believe are broken, neglected, or badly misunderstood."; "Things you would be proud to have helped change."
- "2— Name the work you enjoy doing": "kinds of tasks or work styles you actually enjoy — writing, persuading, designing, organizing, analyzing, connecting people? What skills or experiences do you already have that might be useful in those areas? Are there new ones I'd be excited to build?"
- "3— Form career hypotheses": "Use the form: "I hypothesize I could contribute to [field or problem] by using or developing [skills or interests], possibly through [type of role or organization]."" / "Generate at least three possible career hypotheses. They don't need to be realistic career plans yet." / "Then choose the hypothesis that feels most meaningful and engaging to you. If they all feel equally interesting, just pick one to begin with." / "Then go test it against reality. Go prototype a life."
- Examples (verbatim): "I'm concerned about climate change, and I like working with data. Maybe I could contribute by doing analysis or evaluation for an environmental nonprofit or government agency." / "I love explaining ideas clearly, and I care about access to good information. I wonder if I could work in science communication or public engagement for a research institute."

Closing guidance (verbatim): "You will only know by doing." / "Motion reveals more than introspection ever will." / ""Keeping your options open" is foolish advice. You can live many different lives, but you can only live one at a time." / "Once you choose a path, stop questioning. Make a decision and let go." / "The right path is one whose work holds your curiosity across multiple seasons and slowly hardens into felt purpose."

**Prompt questions (verbatim — the assignment form):**
- "1. Poll" — "Which road feels more like yours right now? Road one or two?"
  - "Road 1: I already feel claimed by something. I need to take my passion more seriously and build mastery, discipline, and worldly value around it."
  - "Road 2: I don't feel claimed by something yet. I need to start with problems I care about and skills I enjoy, and let passion grow through competence and contribution."
- "2. Write your one-line work thesis" — "Please complete this sentence:"
  - "I think I could contribute to [field or problem] by using or developing [skills or interests], possibly through [type of role, project, or organization]."

**Constraints:**
- Poll: single choice, exactly one of two.
- Thesis: one sentence, three bracketed slots: `[field or problem]`, `[skills or interests]`, `[type of role, project, or organization]`.
- Note: the assignment form says "I think I could contribute…" while the Probably Good form in the lecture says "I hypothesize I could contribute…". Use the assignment wording ("I think") in the app.

## 3. Work Life Audit

**Exact name in PDF:** "7th assignment" / "Audit your work life". Lecture title: "The ingredients of a fulfilling work life".

**Purpose / logic (verbatim):** "What are the goods that make up a fulfilling work life? For this section, I'm drawing directly from the research and framework presented by 80,000 Hours..." / "Taken together, these five goods form an incredibly useful checklist — a tool for auditing your own work life to see what you're missing."

**"The five major goods" (verbatim):**
1. "Work that's engaging hour-to-hour"
2. "Work that helps others"
3. "Work you're good at"
4. "Supportive community or colleagues"
5. "A lack of major negatives"

Engagement's "four structural features" (verbatim): "Freedom to decide how to perform your work"; "Clear tasks with a well-defined start and end"; "Variety in the nature of those tasks"; "Feedback, so you know how well you're doing". Key line: "A good work life needs engagement in both substance and structure." / "The question "Do I enjoy doing X?" isn't enough, you want to ask: "Is the way I get to do this work interesting, challenging and agentic?""

Four dissatisfaction factors (verbatim): "Long commutes"; "Very long hours"; "Pay that feels unfair"; "Job insecurity".

"Not all goods are equal" (verbatim): "Two of them — good colleagues and the absence of major negatives — are supporting goods... The other three — engagement, impact, and competence — are the heart. You always want to tip the balance heavily toward these three."

Deeper lesson (verbatim): "whatever you're most lacking will feel like the most important thing in the world." / "A flourishing work life is not built around one good — it's built by having the goods in the right order." / "Many people say, "I hate my job," when what they really hate are certain parts of it."

### Scoring form (verbatim)

"Rate your current work life from 1 to 5 in each area."
"1 = awful"
"5 = excellent"

**1. Day-to-day engagement** — "How engaging is the work itself, hour to hour?" Consider whether:
- "The work itself genuinely interests you."
- "You have the freedom to decide how to perform your work without being micromanaged."
- "Your tasks have clear goals and a well-defined beginning and end."
- "There is enough variety in the work you do."
- "You receive useful feedback and know whether you're doing well."
"Rating: ___ / 5"

**2. Meaning** — "How meaningful does your work feel?" Consider whether:
- "It aligns with your values."
- "It makes a real contribution to other people. It helps others."
- "It helps you become the person you want to be. It serves your ideal self."
"Rating: ___ / 5"

**3. Strengths and growth** — "Does your work call upon your natural strengths and help you develop skills you genuinely care about?" "Rating: ___ / 5"

**4. People and community** — "Do you have supportive colleagues, collaborators or a professional community that gives you a sense of belonging?" "Rating: ___ / 5"

**5. Sustainability** — "Are the practical conditions of your work sustainable?" "Does your work lack major negatives such as unfair pay, long commutes, very long hours, or chronic stress?" "Rating: ___ / 5"

### Interpreting results (verbatim)

"What should you do with your results?"
"This audit should help you decide whether to reshape your current job or pursue a new one."
"Add your five ratings together. Your total score will fall somewhere between 5 and 25."
"This isn't an exact verdict on your career, but it's a useful tool for helping you judge whether your work needs to be reshaped or replaced."
"Remember, however, that not all five goods are equally important, so they shouldn't carry equal weight in your judgment. Day-to-day engagement and meaning matter most."
"If engagement and meaning score highly while the others score poorly, the work itself is likely right for you; you may simply need to reshape your job or find a better environment in which to do it. But if engagement and meaning score poorly while the others score highly, you may have good conditions surrounding work that is fundamentally unfulfilling. In that case, you should seriously consider exploring another job, regardless of your total score."

"Your total score" bands:
- **"22–25: Build on what you have"** — "You already have the foundation of a great work life. Don't create a crisis simply because your work isn't perfect. You want to deepen what is working:" "Spend more time on the parts of your work that give you energy."; "Reduce, delegate or restructure the parts that drain you."; "Seek greater responsibility, mastery and impact."; "Identify the one or two areas keeping your score from being even higher." "You probably don't need a new career. You probably need to shape your present one more deliberately."
- **"18–21: Reshape before you replace"** — "Something substantial is working, but something substantial is missing. Identify your two lowest ratings and run a 30-day experiment aimed directly at improving them. Ask for different responsibilities. Take ownership of a project. Seek more feedback. Reduce one recurring drain. Investigate another team, specialization or employer. Then score yourself again. If the score doesn't rise — or if your lowest scores concern meaning and engagement — you should begin exploring alternative careers."
- **"13–17: Begin actively exploring other possibilities"** — "Your present work life is giving you some important goods, but not enough of them. This is the dangerous middle: tolerable enough to remain in, but not good enough to devote years of your life to. Don't necessarily resign tomorrow. Instead, begin running two tracks at once:" "1. Improve what can be improved in your current work." "2. Develop and test different career hypotheses. See "Road Two Action Plan""
- **"5–12: Begin planning your exit"** — "Your work life is failing to provide most of the goods required for fulfillment. Unless this is the temporary result of an unusual crisis, you should not accept it as your long-term future. Don't leave recklessly, but you should begin an immediate transition."

**Summary of discrepancies (PDF is authoritative):**
1. Order: PDF is Ideal Selves (1) → Wikipedia entry (2) → What pulls you (3) → **Noble emergency kit (4) → Life rules (5)** → 1st practice → Poll + work thesis (6) → Work life audit (7). The app list swaps 4 and 5.

3. Coverage: the app list omits the 6th assignment (road poll + one-line work thesis) and the 7th assignment (work life audit). If the onboarding intentionally stops at identity work, these two should be scheduled as a follow-on "Work" module rather than dropped.
4. The PDF prefixes the 6th and 7th assignments with mandatory reading of an included lecture; the app should surface at least a condensed lecture (or the cues/goods lists) before those forms.
